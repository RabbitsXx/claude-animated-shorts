# What If A Time Machine Actually Worked?

## Facts and sources

- FICTION: travel into the past has no known mechanism; the film states the machine as fiction and only asks what would follow if one worked.
- Earth's mean orbital speed around the Sun is 29.78 km/s (NASA Goddard Earth Fact Sheet; read via secondary sources space.com and WorldAtlas, NASA page not opened directly). Perihelion 30.29, aphelion 29.29 km/s (Wikipedia, Orbit of Earth).
- Derived: 29.78 km/s x 3600 s = 107,208 km per hour; x 86400 s = 2,572,992 km per day (about 6.7 Earth-Moon distances at 384,400 km; Moon distance not shown in film).
- Logic: a machine that changes only the time coordinate returns you to the same position in space at an earlier time. Because Earth moved in the meantime, that point is far from where Earth was then. The film uses the Sun-centred frame for orbital motion; it ignores Earth's rotation and the Sun's motion around the Galaxy (not verified here, not stated in the film). Position is frame-dependent, so the film calls the fix a "planet tracker" and does not claim a real machine could do it.
- Arrival readouts are shown only on the inventor's own model (a table orrery), never as a live feed from the past.
- Real flavour, not in narration: GPS satellite clocks gain about 38 microseconds per day net (about +45 from weaker gravity, about -7 from speed); sources via maketecheasier summaries, authoritative reference is Ashby, Living Reviews in Relativity (not opened).
- Cast and props are original; no third-party art.

## Narration

> He swung the hammer, and the world's first time machine was finished.

> It cost him everything.

> Across the field, a sponsored rival was building a bigger one.

> First test: send a dummy back one hour. The machine hummed, flashed, and the dummy vanished.

> His model said it landed a hundred and seven thousand kilometers from Earth. In empty space.

> Because Earth never sits still. It races around the Sun at thirty kilometers every second. Go back an hour, and your old spot is far behind you.

> It's like stepping off a train, going back a minute, and landing on empty track.

> So he bolted on a planet tracker.

> The next dummy arrived one minute before it left. Now there were two.

> Opening day. Balloons, banners, a huge crowd.

> The rival went first: one whole day back, no tracker, no patience.

> Lever down. Flash. Silence.

> On the model, one tiny man in a bow tie floated two and a half million kilometers from a small blue marble.

> The inventor looked at the crowd, and tightened one last bolt.
